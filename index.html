Abstract

        “Active Learning (Angluin, 1988, Cohn et al., 1994) is a paradigm where a concept is learnt by an
        intelligent machine through the interaction of an oracle with the machine system, over a sequence of
        learning epochs. A distinctive component in an active system is the querying strategy, used to sample
        candidate training instances from a pool and evaluate them for informativeness, i.e., their potential
        to improve the classification model. Various strategies have been proposed including; Query by
        Committee (Seung et al., 1992), Monte Carlo estimation (Roy and McCallum, 2001), uncertainty
        sampling (Lewis and Gale, 1994) and version space reduction (margin sampling) (Tong and Koller,
        2001). Irrespective of how the most informative pattern instances are chosen at each epoch they are
        presented for labeling, to a machine (database) or human oracle that has knowledge of the class
        labels. After adding the newly labeled instances to the current training set, a revised model is
        subsequently induced. A goal of this process is to achieve an efficient, incremental advancement of
        learning at each epoch. The process is continued until some stopping criterion has been met, such as
        reaching a stage where information from the selected instances is contrary to the model, causing the
        classifier confidence to drop, as in Vlachos (2008).

Introduction

        The ocean covers approximately 70% of the Earth’s plane and its surface area is approximately 360 million square kilometres.
        It is estimated that the average depth of the ocean is approximately 3682 metres (NOAA Ocean Exploration, 2013).
        As of June 2025, 27.3 percent of the global seafloors has been mapped using the high-resolution technology of multibeam sonar
        systems (NOAA Ocean Exploration, 2025), however physical human exploration of the deep ocean seafloor through use of
        submersibles is less than 0.001%. This page aims to discuss the advancements and breakthroughs in the design of submersibles
        and technology to allow the progression of exploration of the deep expanse.

Analysis and Discussion

        Challenges faced:
        Due to the nature of the deep-sea making exploration so difficult, until recently, research was conducted almost entirely from the surface.
        One of the main challenges regarding the exploration of the sea is the lack of light that reaches deep areas of the sea, only in depths of
        above 200m – called the ‘sunlight zone’ can marine exploration take place without the involvement of artificial light sources. Once the
        ‘midnight zone’ is reached – depths of over 1000m – all light from the sun is absorbed and scattered by the water resulting in total darkness
        in areas of sea in this region (NOAA Ocean Exploration, n.d.). Furthermore, an additional factor limiting the possible boundaries of
        exploration – particularly on the front of technology – is the issues regarding the pressure experienced in the deep sea induced by the
        increasing weight of the water the further down the depth (reaching pressures of up to 1100 atmospheres)(Encounter Edu, n.d.). The deepest any
        human scuba diver has ever reached without the aid of pressure hulls is 318m
        Foundations of submersibles:
        The first submersible made specifically for exploration was named the Bathysphere. Marine biologist William Beebe and engineer Otis Barton built
        the Bathysphere in 1930 the 5000 lbs cast iron sphere could house 2 divers within and the use of two eight-inch-thick fused quartz portholes
        allowed for observation into the surrounding environment. The Bathysphere was tethered to a ship on the surface and was provided with its necessary
        life supports through a rubber hose. However, the nature of the Bathysphere brought along a list of limitations. The Bathysphere suffered from
        mobility issues as its only mobility options was vertically, and the motions of the ship above played a substantial role in the motions of the
        sphere below. Additionally use of portholes provided 2 limitations, the portholes proved to be an issue with visibility and provided a highly limited
        field of view for the divers inside furthermore the Bathysphere relied entirely on observation and collecting samples was impossible therefore the
        scientific research was severely limited to what could be transmitted verbally between the divers below and the ship’s crew above (Smithsonian Ocean
        Portal, n.d.). While its capabilities where limited the Bathysphere provided the groundwork for further future developments.
        Development following the Bathysphere:
        Following the invention of the Bathysphere Swiss scientist Auguste Piccard and his son Jacques designed the Bathyscaphe designed to be a vessel with
        its own methods of propulsion to explore the depth of the sea. The Bathyscape was built between 1946 and 1948. It was made with heavily reinforced
        steel to weather the extreme pressures of the deep ocean, the walls were approximately 12.7cm thick and had a diameter of 2.16m. Additionally the vessel
        used gasoline to fuel its propulsion as it was lighter than water and incompressible thus providing the submersible with the lift necessary to keep the
        Bathyscaphe afloat. The vessel reached depths of up to 4000 off the coast of Senegal on February 15th, 1954 (Encyclopaedia Britannica, n.d.). An improved
        Bathyscaphe – called the Trieste – was acquired by the US navy this vessel reached depth of 10916 metres in the Mariana Trench.
        Sonar Technologies mapping progress:
        The invention of Sonar (Sound Navigation and Ranging) technology began development in the early 1900s and was heavily accelerated by the sinking of the
        Titanic in 1912. Its primary use was to prevent submarine warfare in the First world war buts its use after the war was a huge impact to aid the exploration
        of the deep sea. Active sonar system uses an audio frequency that echoes off objects and environments and the measured time between the emitting and the
        returning of the pulse can be used to measure distances and therefore map out the geographic properties of the seabed allowed for a huge breakthrough in the
        exploration in areas that were unsuitable for human or even submersible involvement. Passive Sonar systems remain stationary and monitors underwater sounds
        from the direct vicinity of the receiver allowing the systems to detect the presence of sea creatures and monitor the activities of nearby marine life, passive
        sonar systems could be deployed in areas of high pressure and as pressures of the water does not prevent the movement of sound. These technologies enable
        gathering of information from areas far beyond the reach of divers or submersibles (Encounter Edu, n.d.).
